# One reading of company names, windows and edits, whichever planner proposed them

> **Extends [ADR 0002](0002-lookup-membership.md) and [ADR 0004](0004-ambiguous-metric-clarify.md).** The planner still only proposes; resolution still decides.

The planner comparison found three cases that both planners failed, and three more that only the LLM planner failed. None was a planning error. Each was in the deterministic code that runs after either planner:

- **Two resolvers.** The rules planner read names with the issuer index, but spec resolution read a planner's company field with a narrower SEC-style resolver. So "Goldman Sachs", "Lilly" and "Merck and Co" from the LLM planner were not found. Facts were then fetched by the raw text, and resolved a third time from SEC titles: GS was shown as "company not found", and "Coca-Cola" came back ambiguous between three bottlers.
- **A narrow period reader.** It knew "last N quarters" for six number words. "Past six quarters" and "previous nine quarters" fell back to the latest quarter.
- **Edits read from raw text.** "include Oracle too" became a company called "Oracle too", and "what about Goldman?" meant a swap only to the rules planner.

## Decision

**One resolver.** Spec resolution reads a company field with the planner's issuer index, and the facts lookup is given the snapshot's reading of a name before SEC titles. A ticker typed as one ("TEAM", "$COKE") is that listing even where it is also a name. The index also takes the names the 1,500 largest companies used to file under (SEC submissions), and a listing's security description ("Class A Common Stock", "S.A.B. de C.V.") is not part of the name. The intent follows the companies left on screen once names are resolved: a comparison that ends with one, after a name no company matched or a fund left out with a note ("SPY and Apple revenue", "Google and Alphabet revenue"), is a lookup (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 03).

**A name that is also a word.** Whether a word is everyday English comes from case in 10-Q text: filings write "target", "block" and "match" in lower case mid-sentence, and "Nvidia" and "Oracle" with a capital (`scripts/build_everyday_words.py`). An everyday-word name counts only where the question uses it as a company: capitalised mid-sentence, possessive, listed with another company, followed by a figure, a company's verb or a legal form. A capitalised-in-filings name counts unless the question plainly uses the word ("ask the oracle"). Some uses of the word are read as the word whatever the case: "intel" as information (`intel aside`, `any intel`), "micron" as a unit (`to the micron`, `a micron`), `the apple of`, `an oracle for`, and idioms such as `apples to apples`. These are read where both planners pass, so a company either planner proposes from such a word is dropped unless the question also names it (`Intel and Palantir`) (revised 7 October 2026, held-out-5-findings ticket 02). A segment only one company reports names that company when the question names none ("iPhone sales" is Apple's, "Azure revenue" Microsoft's), read where both planners pass and in the rules planner's plan, so the cascade keeps it; the answer is the company-wide figure with the segment note, which the shared answer notes add whichever planner planned (revised 7 October 2026, held-out-5-findings ticket 03).

**A name several companies share is asked about.** "Lincoln" is Lincoln Electric or Lincoln National. The answer offers the snapshot's largest matches, and the analyst's reply (a number, a ticker or a name) resumes the held question. Guessing the larger company would answer a different question with confidence.

**One period grammar.** A window is a recency word or preposition, a count and a unit; after the metric the recency word may be left out ("Apple revenue 18 months", "6 quarters", "a couple of years" are the same windows; revised 6 October 2026, review-69-90 ticket 04). A count that names something else stays what it names: "3 months ended June" and "2 quarters ago" name a quarter, and "12-month" in "trailing 12-month revenue" is the trailing year. Counts run to ninety-nine in digits or words; "a couple" is 2 and "a dozen" 12. "A few" and "several" read as 4, and the answer says so. Years are four quarters; months are a third of one, rounded up, and said. A whole number of years and a half ("a year and a half", "one and a half years", "2.5 years") is that many years and two quarters more; a decimal that is not a half is left unread rather than read by its last digits (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 01). A model planner may propose a window too (`recent_quarters`). The grammar's reading wins, and the model's stands only where the grammar reads none and the message has a period word at all. So "what about AMD?" cannot pick up a window nobody asked for. A "since" window ("since 2024") is every filed quarter that ended on or after 1 January of that year, on each company's own calendar, counted from the filings rather than today; named as a fiscal year ("since the start of fiscal 2025", "since FY2025"), it is every quarter of each company's own fiscal year and after, read where its fiscal periods are listed as a named fiscal year's quarters are, so Apple's opens with the quarter ended December 2024 and Microsoft's with September 2024 (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 06). The year of a "since" window is a year, not a count: "since 2025 year over year" is the window since 2025 began with a change on it, not 2025 years (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 10).

**Growth is year over year; a change with no base is asked about.** Analysts compare a quarter with the same quarter a year earlier, because a season does not distort that comparison. 10-Q MD&A has long done the same (Regulation S-K Item 303(c), which since 2020 also allows the quarter before). So "growth", "grew" and "trend" mean year over year, and the answer says so. "Sequential", "quarter over quarter" and "vs last quarter" mean the quarter before. Wording that asks about a change but names no base ("why did revenue drop?", "how has revenue changed?") is asked about first: "Compared with what? The same quarter a year earlier, or the quarter before?" Apple's revenue in the quarter to June 2026 fell 1.6% from the quarter before and rose 16.4% from a year earlier. Answered year over year, "why did it drop?" would contradict the analyst. After a view with one change, "sequential instead", "quarter over quarter instead" and "make it sequential" switch it to the quarter before and keep the quarters on screen, reading the quarter before the oldest as its base without showing it; "year over year instead" switches back the same way (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 05). A change over a named window ("how did EBITDA change over the past year?", "grow over the last 2 years") is year over year over that window: a year named with no count is its four quarters, as "growth over the last 4 quarters" already was, not the five of growth with no period (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 07). That holds whatever the change wording: "how much did revenue change over the last year?" and "over the past 10 quarters, how has revenue moved?" read as "how did revenue change over the past year?" does, rather than showing the span's quarters with no change column for the analyst to subtract; with no window, the same words still ask against what (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 09). A "since" window is a window too: "how much did revenue change since 2023?" and "revenue growth since 2024" are every filed quarter since the year began, each with its year-over-year change from its own comparative, rather than the quarters alone (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 10). Naming both bases ("sequentially or versus last year", "quarter over quarter and year over year") shows both changes on every quarter shown, wherever the bases sit in the question: the analysis carries a `sequential` operation beside `year_over_year`, so each quarter's year-over-year change comes from its own comparative as a year-over-year question's does, not only where the year-earlier quarter happens to be on screen, and its change on the quarter before stays beside it (revised 7 October 2026, held-out-5-findings ticket 01).

**A ranking's group and order.** Everyday names for a group name its industry in the snapshot's group aliases, which both planners' rankings resolve through ("semis", "chipmakers", "drugmakers", "big pharma", "big banks"). A group and "by" a metric with no "top" is a ranking of the default length. The order's direction is read from the words for both planners, as periods are: "lowest first", "smallest first" and "ascending" run the same members, still the largest by market value, from the lowest value of the metric; "largest first" turns it back, and it holds across edits until another order is named. "Bottom 5" stays refused: rankings start from the largest (revised 7 October 2026, probe-round-3-gaps ticket 04).

**Edits by their own words.** For both planners, "add", "include", "too" and "as well" add companies, and adding never removes. "What about", "how about" and "same for" put the named companies in place of those on screen, keeping metrics and window; naming a metric and no company ("what about net income", "just net income", "net income instead"), they put the metric in place of those on screen, keeping companies and window. "Their" or "its" ("what about their net income") asks about the same companies, and adds. "Swap X for Y" swaps. "Switch the metric to X" and "show X instead" replace the metric. An edit's companies are read with the index, so filler never becomes a company.

**Evaluation discipline.** Cases a planner change was diagnosed on are development cases. A held-out set is written by a separate session after the change, labelled with the product's behaviour, and not read by whoever changed the planner until it has run. A window is scored by the quarters asked for, since the recording holds about nine quarters a company and the answer already says when it shows fewer.

## Considered options

- **Teach the LLM planner more aliases in its prompt.** Rejected: the failures were after planning, and the rules planner, which plans wherever no OpenAI key is set, would not benefit.
- **Resolve every name to the largest matching company.** Rejected: it answers "Lincoln revenue" for a company the analyst may not mean, with no sign that it chose.
- **A hand-written list of everyday-word company names.** Rejected: it goes stale as the snapshot changes. Case in filings is measured, and rebuilt by a script.
- **Show both changes whenever the base is unclear.** Rejected: a table with both columns still leaves the analyst to work out which one answers the question. One short question is clearer, and the answer then leads with the change that was asked about.
- **Let the model's window win when it disagrees with the wording.** Rejected: the wording is what the analyst typed, and the grammar's reading is deterministic and tested.
